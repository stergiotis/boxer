# Closed-loop management — the practice boxer strives to support, and where the evidence sits

Organisations manage many things — risk, quality, projects, portfolios,
supply, talent, money, compliance — and each has its own "x-management"
discipline with its own frameworks. Read for outcome evidence, those
disciplines turn out to share one structure and one pattern. The structure is
a control loop applied to a different object each time. The pattern is that
evidence that a practice improves outcomes attaches to particular **steps of
that loop**, almost never to the named framework around them.

This page states both. Together they describe the kind of management
practice boxer strives to support optimally: the loop run **closed** —
outcomes recorded and read back into the next goal, judgement and
allocation — with effort weighted toward the steps where the evidence is
strongest. The premises in [why-boxer](./why-boxer.md) say how boxer is
built; this page says what kind of management that build is meant to serve.

## Vocabulary

| Word | Meaning here |
| --- | --- |
| practice | anything an organisation does routinely to steer an outcome; the umbrella term |
| mechanism | one step of the loop done in a specific, testable way — a structured interview, a randomised inspection, a debrief. Evidence attaches here |
| framework | a named bundle of mechanisms with a vocabulary, often a certification and a market — ERM, Six Sigma, Stage-Gate, a maturity model |
| closed loop | a practice whose outputs meet an observed outcome and change the next cycle |
| open loop | a practice whose outputs — a register, a score, a certificate — are produced and filed but never meet an outcome |

**Why "closed-loop management".** The term names the operating pattern and
its failure mode in one phrase. Its established neighbours each cover part of
it:

- **Management control** — Anthony's (1965) separation of strategic planning,
  management control and operational control, and Simons's (1995) four
  *levers of control*: belief, boundary, diagnostic and interactive systems.
  Diagnostic control is the goal–measure–correct part of the loop;
  interactive control is the judge–learn part; boundary systems are the
  assure part. Belief systems are outside what a data system can carry.
- **PDCA** (plan–do–check–act, Shewhart and Deming) — the same loop, stated
  for process improvement.
- **Evidence-based management** (Pfeffer & Sutton 2006;
  [CEBMa](https://cebma.org/)) — the stance of choosing practices by the
  quality of evidence behind them. It is a stance on *which* steps to invest
  in, not a description of the loop.

Boxer aims at both halves: the loop run closed, and its steps weighted by
evidence.

## The loop

```text
            ┌──────────────────────── learn ◀─────────────────────────┐
            ▼                                                          │
   set goals ──▶ measure ──▶ judge ──▶ allocate ──▶ execute ──▶ assure ─┘
       ▲                                                          │
       └──────────────── structure and oversee (the frame) ───────┘
```

Every x-management discipline is this loop with a different object in it:
risk management puts exposures in it, quality management defects, portfolio
management investments, talent management people. A discipline is
recognisable by its object; whether it works is decided step by step.

## Where the evidence sits

Strength is graded by the best design behind the step: **experimental**
(randomised or natural experiment on outcomes), **meta-analytic** (many
studies, at the level of individual tasks or decisions), **correlational**
(associations, selection unresolved), **analytical** (a mathematical or
logical argument), or **none found**.

| Step | Evidence | What is established | What fails |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Set goals** | Meta-analytic and natural-experiment | Specific, hard goals with feedback raise task performance across well over a hundred tasks (Locke & Latham 2002). English hospital waiting-time targets backed by public reporting and sanctions cut waits relative to Scotland, which had targets without them (Propper et al. 2010) | The gaming is as reliable as the gain: patients removed from lists, activity shifted to what is counted (Bevan & Hood 2006; Propper et al. 2010) |
| **Measure** | Meta-analytic, mixed | Some signals predict well: the cumulative cost-performance index of defence contracts stabilises early (Christensen & Heise 1993) | Feedback raises performance on average (d ≈ 0.41) yet lowers it in over a third of interventions (Kluger & DeNisi 1996). Many managed metrics are poorly validated: major ESG ratings correlate only 0.38–0.71 with each other (Berg, Kölbel & Rigobon 2022); observational advertising measurement often fails to recover the effects field experiments measure, and tends to overstate them (Gordon et al. 2019) |
| **Judge** | Meta-analytic and experimental | Mechanical prediction — a formula, a scoring rule — is on average about 10% more accurate than clinical judgement, and equal or better in the large majority of 136 comparisons (Meehl 1954; Grove et al. 2000). Structured interviews are the strongest single predictor of job performance (Sackett et al. 2022). Managers who overrode a hiring test hired people who left sooner (Hoffman, Kahn & Li 2018). Unaided experts are mostly miscalibrated, and performance-weighted aggregation beats equal weighting (Colson & Cooke 2017, 2018) | Intuitive, unstructured judgement — the default in most review boards and risk workshops |
| **Allocate** | Analytical and observational for the biases; none found for the processes | Cost underestimation in about nine of ten of 258 transport projects, with no improvement over 70 years (Flyvbjerg, Holm & Buhl 2002); reference-class forecasting as the correction, adopted as optimism-bias uplifts by the UK Department for Transport (Flyvbjerg 2006). Escalation of commitment to failing courses of action is well documented (Sleesman et al. 2012) | Portfolio boards, stage gates and scoring grids built to apply these corrections have practitioner benchmarking behind them, not outcome evidence |
| **Execute** | Experimental | Randomised management consulting raised productivity 17% in the first year in Indian textile plants (Bloom et al. 2013); a similar trial in Mexico raised productivity and, five years on, employment (Bruhn, Karlan & Schoar 2018); post-war US management training of Italian firms had effects lasting at least 15 years (Giorcelli 2019). Across US plants, structured management accounts for over 20% of productivity variation, as much as R&D, ICT or human capital (Bloom et al. 2019). Piece rates raised output 44% where output was measurable (Lazear 2000). A checklist and culture intervention cut ICU catheter infections from a median 2.7 per 1,000 catheter-days to zero (Pronovost et al. 2006, a cohort study) | The same surgical checklist that preceded halved mortality in eight hospitals (Haynes et al. 2009) showed no significant effect after province-wide, policy-driven adoption across 101 Ontario hospitals (Urbach et al. 2014) |
| **Assure** | Experimental for random, consequential assurance; correlational for certification | Randomised workplace safety inspections cut injuries 9.4% and injury costs 26% with no detectable job loss (Levine, Toffel & Johnson 2012). Random municipal audits in Brazil reduced later corruption by about 8% (Avis, Ferraz & Finan 2018) | ISO 9001 adopters survived at higher rates, and grew faster among survivors, than matched non-adopters, but adoption is chosen (Levine & Toffel 2010). Assurance shaped by "the logic of the audit trail" produces records that defend the organisation rather than steer it (Power 2009, 2021) |
| **Learn** | Meta-analytic and experimental | Debriefs and after-action reviews improve team performance by roughly 20–25% (Tannenbaum & Cerasoli 2013). Organisational learning curves are among the most replicated findings in operations (Argote & Epple 1990). Entrepreneurs taught to treat strategy as hypotheses to test performed better in a randomised trial (Camuffo et al. 2020); start-ups adopting A/B testing grew faster in observational data (Koning, Hasan & Chatterji 2022) | Lessons-learned registers that no later cycle reads — open loop by construction |
| **Structure and oversee** | Meta-analytic, mostly null | — | Meta-analyses of board composition and leadership structure find little systematic relationship with financial performance (Dalton et al. 1998). Governance indices that once predicted returns stopped doing so once markets learned them (Bebchuk, Cohen & Wang 2013) |

Two results cut across every row. Where practices have been put to
randomised trial, the trial has overturned popular practice about as often as
it has confirmed it: workplace wellness programmes showed little or no effect
on health, spending or productivity in two trials (Jones, Molitor & Reif 2019;
Song & Baicker 2019). And effects shrink at scale: interventions run by
government nudge units averaged 1.4 percentage points, against 8.7 in
academic journals (DellaVigna & Linos 2022).

## Regularities

What follows is a reading of the table, not a further finding. It is meant
to hold for as long as the evidence above does.

**R1 — Evidence attaches to mechanisms, not frameworks.** No framework in
the table carries outcome evidence of its own. Where a framework appears to
work, the mechanisms inside it — a structured judgement, a checklist at the
point of work, a debrief — are the plausible carriers. Maturity models,
present in nearly every discipline, measure whether a process is followed,
which is the one thing the evidence does not reward.

**R2 — How a practice is adopted decides whether it works.** The surgical
checklist is the clean case: the same instrument, with an effect when
clinicians took it up and none detected when a policy spread it. Power's
audit-trail argument is the general form. A practice adopted to change
behaviour at the point of hazard and a practice adopted to produce a record
for a reviewer are different practices with the same name.

**R3 — Evidence exists where feedback loops are short and measurable.**
Hiring retention, forecast accuracy, defect and infection rates, output per
hour: these can be measured and experimented on, and they carry the strong
results. Culture, governance, enterprise risk and change programmes have
long, confounded loops, and carry the weak ones. Part of the gap is
structural — rare outcomes, invisible counterfactuals, no randomisation —
so the absence of evidence there is not evidence of absence. It is still
absence.

**R4 — Unpredictable, consequential assurance works; predictable,
self-reported assurance is unproven.** Random inspections and random audits
have experimental support. Scheduled self-assessment, certification and
attestation have correlational support at best.

**R5 — Measurement moves what it measures, in both directions.** Targets
reliably move the targeted number and reliably induce gaming; feedback
helps on average and harms often. A measure needs a check on the measure.

**R6 — Effects found elsewhere shrink when transplanted.** A practice's
published effect is an upper bound on what it will do in a new setting. The
operation that measures its own practices is the only one that learns what
they do for it — which makes evaluation the step with the best track record
of all, and the one the loop most often omits.
